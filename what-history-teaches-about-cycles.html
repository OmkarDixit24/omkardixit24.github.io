What History Teaches About Cycles

History does not repeat, but the psychology that drives cycles does. Credit expands, narratives grow more optimistic, risk is underpriced, and then the reverse occurs. The names and the instruments change. The sequence is familiar.

Studying past cycles is less about finding exact analogues and more about recognising the emotional and institutional patterns that recur. Excess confidence, the gradual normalisation of leverage, the dismissal of caution as outdated—these are features, not bugs, of how humans coordinate around risk.

The limits of analogy

Every cycle has unique elements. Treating 1929, 2000, or 2008 as templates that will replay on schedule is a category error. The value of history is in the questions it trains you to ask: Where is leverage building? Which assumptions are treated as permanent? Who is being paid to stay optimistic?

Primary documents from past cycles—letters, reports, contemporary commentary— are especially useful. They capture how the story felt while it was still open, before hindsight smoothed it into inevitability.

The most useful history is the kind that makes the present feel less unprecedented and your own certainty feel less permanent.

Cycles teach humility about timing and respect for the durability of certain human tendencies. They do not hand you a map. They hand you a better set of questions and a longer memory.
