The Compound Effect of Better Questions

Most people optimise for answers. The better investment of attention is usually in the quality of the questions you ask. A slightly better question, repeated over years, compounds in the same way capital does—quietly at first, then decisively.

In markets this shows up clearly. Two analysts can look at the same company and reach opposite conclusions not because one has more data, but because one is asking a more precise question about what actually drives value. The difference is rarely dramatic on day one. Over a decade it becomes the entire edge.

Questions as filters

A good question acts as a filter. It discards noise before it reaches your working memory. “Is this a good business?” is almost useless. “Under what conditions does this business’s competitive advantage erode?” forces specificity. The second question changes what you notice in filings, calls, and industry conversations.

The same pattern appears outside investing. In conversations, in research, in deciding what to work on next—the people who get further are often those who treat question design as a craft rather than an afterthought.

The quality of your outcomes is downstream of the quality of the questions you are willing to sit with.

Better questions are not more complicated questions. They are usually simpler, more concrete, and harder to answer honestly. They resist the urge to collapse uncertainty into a narrative that feels complete.

If there is one habit worth building, it is the habit of rewriting the question before rushing to the answer. The rewrite is where most of the leverage lives.
