The Cost of Certainty

Certainty feels like strength. In practice it is often a costly form of rigidity. The more certain you become about a view, the more you pay—in attention, in missed alternatives, and in the eventual cost of being wrong.

Markets reward calibrated confidence and punish both paralysis and overconfidence. The expensive version of certainty is the one that stops you from updating when the evidence shifts. You keep paying for a belief that is no longer earning its keep.

Leaving room

Leaving room for being wrong is not the same as lack of conviction. It is the difference between a view you hold and a view that holds you. The former can be revised. The latter requires a crisis.

The highest cost of certainty is the set of questions you stop asking.

A practical check: for any strong view, write down what would make you reduce or abandon it. If the list is empty or vague, the certainty has already started to cost more than it is worth.
