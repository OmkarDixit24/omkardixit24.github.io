On Intellectual Humility in Markets

Intellectual humility is easy to endorse and hard to practice when money is on the line. Markets punish both arrogance and excessive doubt. The useful middle path is calibrated confidence: strong enough to act, soft enough to update.

Humility here does not mean low conviction. It means knowing the difference between what the data can support and what your narrative is filling in. Most costly mistakes come from treating the latter as if it were the former.

Update as a skill

The market does not care how long you have held a view. It only cares whether the view still maps to reality. The ability to change your mind without rewriting your entire self-image is a competitive advantage that compounds.

One practical test: can you articulate the strongest version of the opposing case better than its advocates? If not, your confidence is partly an artifact of incomplete exposure rather than superior insight.

Certainty is a feeling. Calibration is a practice.

Keeping a decision journal—recording the thesis, the key uncertainties, and the conditions that would change the view—turns humility from a slogan into a process. Over time the journal becomes a mirror. It shows you where your confidence has been well-placed and where it has been theatre.
